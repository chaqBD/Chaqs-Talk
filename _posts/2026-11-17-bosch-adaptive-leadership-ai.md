Today's paper: a six-year case study of Robert Bosch GmbH's experience running
more than 30 AI projects in operations and supply chain management, examined
through the lens of adaptive leadership theory.

## What the study found

Drawing on six years of research across 30+ projects and interviews with 10
managers at Bosch, the authors find that leadership involvement helps or hurts
AI adoption depending on how well it's matched to where a project actually
sits in its lifecycle — not on how much leaders get involved in absolute terms.

## Six lessons

1. **Micromanagement can undermine AI implementation.** In an early
   maintenance project, managers' involvement in daily technical decisions
   restricted experimentation and slowed delivery. Leadership enthusiasm
   became a source of rigidity — greater involvement does not automatically
   produce better outcomes.
2. **Leadership must change as AI projects mature.** The study identifies
   three phases: structuring, where leaders provide resources and protect
   experimentation; implementation, where they actively coordinate
   departments; and scaling, where they concentrate on strategy and
   performance oversight.
3. **A promising pilot can remain operationally isolated.** Bosch's early
   predictive-maintenance proof of concept showed potential to anticipate
   tool failures but was never integrated into a wider maintenance strategy.
   Poor data quality, inconsistent formats, and departmental silos blocked
   the move from technical experiment to broader application.
4. **Cross-functional coordination helps convert AI into operational
   improvements.** Bosch established a digital excellence committee
   connecting production, logistics, IT, and maintenance. Computer vision
   applications then improved component identification and routing,
   reducing manual errors and supporting more reliable internal material
   flows.
5. **Supply chain AI integration depends on partners' digital readiness.**
   Supplier integration was delayed because partner firms used different
   systems and had uneven digital capabilities, compounded by conflicting
   departmental priorities. Technical capability inside one organization
   can't, by itself, guarantee connected supply chain data.
6. **Scaling AI introduces new financial and governance responsibilities.**
   Cloud services and pre-trained models supported invoice processing and
   document analysis, but also brought subscription costs, API charges, and
   dependence on external providers — expanding leadership's job to include
   evaluating long-term cost and maintaining strategic alignment.

## How leadership adapted across the three phases

Lesson 2 is really about *knowing when to step in, how to contribute, and
when to step back* — adjusting the form of involvement to the project's
maturity rather than keeping one leadership style throughout:

- **Structuring** — leaders provided resources and gave technical teams room
  to experiment, staying out of day-to-day technical calls.
- **Implementation** — they became more actively involved in coordinating
  departments and resolving integration problems.
- **Scaling** — they shifted to strategic oversight, letting experienced
  teams manage technical delivery on their own.

> Initial AI adoption did not yield immediate improvements in logistics and
> operations and, in some cases, can be detrimental when leadership is
> actively involved.
{: .pull-quote}

## Why it matters

Pair this with [yesterday's Starbucks case]({{ '/2026/11/16/starbucks-ai-inventory-rollback/' | relative_url }}),
and a pattern starts to show up across very different AI-in-supply-chain
failures and successes: the technology itself is rarely the whole story.
Starbucks' problem was a governance mismatch between error consequence and
verification burden; Bosch's is a governance mismatch between leadership
involvement and project maturity. In both cases, the thing that needed to
adapt wasn't the model — it was the organization around it.
